\chapter{Introduction to FoodSense AI}
Food waste is a critical global challenge that impacts food security, the environment, and the economy. This chapter introduces the context of food spoilage detection, the need for automated systems, and outlines the objectives and methodology of the FoodSense AI project.

\section[Introduction]{\textbf{Introduction}}
Food waste is a pressing global issue, with approximately 19\% of all food produced annually being discarded before consumption, as reported by the United Nations Environment Programme. This waste contributes to greenhouse gas emissions, economic losses, and exacerbates food insecurity in many regions. Traditional methods of assessing food freshness rely on manual inspection, which is subjective, labor-intensive, and prone to human error.

Automated food spoilage detection systems have emerged as a solution to this problem, leveraging sensors, machine learning, and computer vision to assess food quality. However, existing systems often suffer from several limitations: they use expensive electronic noses with multiple sensors, provide only binary "fresh vs rotten" classifications without temporal context, and lack on-device sensor compensation.

The proposed system aims to address these limitations by developing a low-cost IoT + AI pipeline for early food spoilage detection. The system uses a combination of MQ135 gas sensor, DHT11 temperature and humidity sensor, Arduino microcontroller, and two AI models: an MLP for gas classification and an LSTM for continuous shelf-life prediction.

\section[Literature Review]{\textbf{Literature Review}}
A literature review was conducted to understand the current state of research in food spoilage detection and identify technical gaps.

\subsection{Gas Sensor-Based Spoilage Detection and Drift Compensation}
Multiple studies have explored the use of metal-oxide gas sensor arrays (electronic noses) for food spoilage detection. Vergara et al. \cite{UCI2016} established a benchmark for analyzing sensor response over a 36-month period, demonstrating that sensor drift is a significant bottleneck in long-term deployment. Traditional methods of drift compensation often rely on batch post-processing in lab settings. However, real-time deployment requires active, localized compensation. Huerta et al. \cite{Fonollosa2015} demonstrated that temperature and humidity fluctuations are primary drivers of drift in open-sampling systems, emphasizing the need for mathematical corrections to stabilize gas readings. 

In recent years, on-device calibration and machine learning have been applied to this domain. Yi et al. \cite{Yi2022} proposed discriminant subspace learning algorithms for drift compensation in gas sensor arrays, while Chang et al. \cite{Hosseinmardi2023} analyzed spatial and temporal drift artifacts in public gas datasets. For hardware implementation, Guo et al. \cite{Karadag2020} classified fruit defects using an electronic nose, and Ricci et al. \cite{Impedance2023} focused on machine learning-based sensing for food quality monitoring. The proposed system builds on this by implementing localized temperature-humidity correction formulas directly on the edge microcontroller.

\subsection{Visible Spectroscopy and Colorimetric Analysis}
Visible spectroscopy provides a non-destructive method for food quality assessment by monitoring chemical and pigment alterations. Fodor et al. \cite{Sun2018} reviewed the use of near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy, showing that absorption at specific wavelengths correlates directly with the degradation of chlorophyll and the formation of brown enzymatic byproducts (melanins). Cort{\'e}s et al. \cite{NonDestructiveChlorophyll2022} reviewed non-destructive monitoring strategies for agricultural products using Vis-NIR spectroscopy.

While commercial spectrophotometers are cost-prohibitive for consumer storage, Albert et al. \cite{Albert2019} demonstrated that Arduino-controlled RGB LEDs and photodetectors (such as LDRs) can perform quantitative chemical analysis. To improve accuracy, Al-Sabbagh and Abdulrazzaq \cite{Wang2024} developed a low-cost RGB LED spectrophotometer, and Nguyen \cite{Nguyen2024} designed continuous ripeness detection algorithms for automatic fruit sorting systems. Recently, Ren et al. \cite{VirtualCalibration2023} demonstrated virtual spectrophotometry calibration via modular 3D-printed spectrometers. The proposed system applies a machine learning-based calibration model to map cheap RGB-LDR signals to continuous wavelengths and absorbances, enabling a low-cost virtual spectrometer.

\subsection{Multimodal Deep Learning and Temporal Modeling}
Traditional AI systems for food quality rely on single-mode point-in-time classification. Amin et al. \cite{Palakodati2020} used CNN architectures for automatic fruit freshness classification. Gao et al. \cite{VisionBasedTransformer2023} investigated food image classification using Vision Transformers. However, static images and gas snapshots lack temporal context. 

Spoilage is a sequential process characterized by progressive VOC releases. While standard LSTMs have been used for time-series forecasting, attention mechanisms are critical for identifying key transient events in sensor readings. Wu et al. \cite{AttentionRNNFood2022} proposed tangerine quality prediction using a graph CNN, LSTM, and attention mechanism. Similarly, Doganis et al. \cite{AttentionLSTMForecasting2023} developed neural network models for forecasting the sales of short shelf-life food products. Edge deployment is addressed by Saha et al. \cite{SmartIoTEdgeAI2022} who designed an IoT-based fruit quality inspection and lifespan detection system, and Abbasi et al. \cite{EdgeAIReview2022} who reviewed digitization and frameworks for smart agriculture. For integration, Di Rosa et al. \cite{MultiModalSensorFusion2023} analyzed multi-modal sensor fusion for animal source food authentication and quality assessment, and Davidescu et al. \cite{IoTEnabledSmartPackaging2020} explored smart packaging technologies. The proposed system introduces a multimodal architecture that runs parallel MLP and attention-augmented LSTM branches, allowing both instant state classification and continuous shelf-life prediction.

\subsection{Fundamental Hardware and Analog/RF Interfaces}
To interface sensors with microcontrollers, fundamental analog signal conditioning is required. Razavi \cite{Razavi2000} outlined the core principles of analog CMOS integrated circuits, which govern sensor readouts and amplifier design. For data transmission, Lu et al. \cite{Lu2023} designed high-throughput dual-band wireless transceivers supporting modern Wi-Fi standards. These foundations allow the proposed system to reliably condition sensor signals and transmit telemetry.

\section[Motivation]{\textbf{Motivation}}
The motivation for developing FoodSense AI stems from several key factors:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The need to reduce food waste and improve food security
    \item The high cost of existing electronic nose systems, which limits accessibility
    \item The lack of temporal context in binary classification systems
    \item The opportunity to apply modern AI techniques like LSTMs with attention to this domain
\end{itemize}

\section[Problem statement]{\textbf{Problem statement}}
The problem this project addresses is the lack of a low-cost, accessible, and accurate system for continuous food spoilage detection that provides temporal context and shelf-life prediction, rather than just binary classification.

\section[Objectives]{\textbf{Objectives}}
The objectives of the project are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item To develop a low-cost IoT system using Arduino, MQ135, and DHT11 sensors for food spoilage detection
    \item To implement on-device temperature and humidity compensation for the MQ135 sensor to obtain an estimated ammonia-sensitive VOC concentration
    \item To train and evaluate an MLP model for gas classification and an LSTM model with temporal attention for shelf-life prediction
\end{enumerate}

\section[Brief Methodology of the project]{\textbf{Brief Methodology of the project}}
The methodology for FoodSense AI involves several key steps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hardware setup: Connect MQ135 and DHT11 sensors to Arduino and implement on-device compensation
    \item Data collection: Generate synthetic time-series data for LSTM training (as a proof-of-concept evaluation) and use the UCI Gas Sensor Array Drift Dataset for MLP training
    \item Model training: Train MLP for classification and LSTM with attention for regression
    \item Evaluation: Test models on appropriate datasets and report metrics
    \item UI development: Create a web-based dashboard using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript
\end{itemize}

\section[Assumptions made / Constraints of the project]{\textbf{Assumptions made / Constraints of the project}}
The project makes the following assumptions and operates under the following constraints:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The MQ135 sensor output is processed to estimate ammonia-sensitive VOC concentration
    \item The synthetic time-series data serves as a proof-of-concept for modeling spoilage dynamics
    \item The system is intended for use in controlled indoor environments
    \item The vision branch is deployed on the server backend using a pre-trained Vision Transformer and fine-tuned CNN
\end{itemize}
