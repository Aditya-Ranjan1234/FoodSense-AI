\begin{abstract}
Food spoilage accounts for approximately 19\% of global food production
loss annually, imposing severe economic and environmental burdens.
Existing detection systems rely on expensive multi-sensor electronic
noses or yield only binary classifications without temporal forecasting.
This paper presents \textit{FoodSense}, a low-cost multimodal sensing and
inference framework that integrates: (i)~an MQ135 metal-oxide gas sensor
with closed-form on-edge temperature--humidity drift compensation
executed on an Arduino ATmega328P microcontroller; (ii)~a DIY
RGB-LED/LDR visible spectrophotometer for non-destructive pigment-level
analysis; and (iii)~a deep visual analysis branch employing a
fine-tuned convolutional neural network (CNN) and Vision Transformer
(ViT). A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) classifier trained on the UCI Gas
Sensor Array Drift Dataset (13,910 samples) achieves 96.84\% accuracy
and an F1-score of 87.28\% for volatile organic compound (VOC) elevation
detection. An attention-augmented LSTM provides continuous shelf-life
regression with an MAE of $\pm$4.0~hours on a proof-of-concept synthetic
time-series. A virtual spectrometer calibration MLP maps RGB-LDR signals
to visible wavelengths with $R^2{=}0.9625$. A pigment-degradation
classifier distinguishes Fresh, Ripe, and Spoiled states at 95.60\%
accuracy. Together, these branches constitute a unified, sub-\$30
hardware platform capable of real-time freshness assessment without
laboratory infrastructure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization estimates that
approximately 19\% of the global food supply is lost each year to
spoilage at the consumer and retail stages, representing a loss of
roughly 931 million tonnes of food annually~\cite{DiRosa2017}.
Beyond nutritional waste, this figure carries a carbon footprint
equivalent to 3.3 billion tonnes of CO$_2$ per year and inflicts
economic damage exceeding USD 1 trillion globally. In developing
economies, inadequate cold-chain infrastructure exacerbates these
losses, making non-refrigerated, sensor-based early-warning systems
particularly valuable.

At a biochemical level, food spoilage proceeds through three
interacting pathways. Microbial metabolism releases volatile organic
compounds (VOCs) including ammonia, hydrogen sulfide, and
trimethylamine into the headspace above deteriorating produce.
Simultaneously, enzymatic oxidation of phenolic compounds drives
non-enzymatic browning, visually manifested as pigment shift from
green chlorophyll to yellow-brown carotenoids and melanins. Finally,
in climacteric fruits, ethylene biosynthesis accelerates respiration
and cell-wall degradation. Each of these pathways produces a
measurable, modality-distinct signal---gas-phase chemistry, reflectance
spectrum, and surface texture---motivating a multimodal sensing
approach.

Existing automated food quality systems suffer from one or more of the
following limitations. Commercially deployed electronic noses (e-noses)
typically employ 8--32 sensor arrays coupled with principal component
analysis or support-vector machines~\cite{Albert2012}, but retail
units routinely cost USD 1,000--15,000, placing them beyond reach for
small-scale food retailers. Single-modality vision systems~\cite{Amin2023}
cannot detect VOC accumulation in sealed packaging. Gas-only systems
ignore visual and spectroscopic cues that manifest earlier in the
spoilage cascade. Crucially, the vast majority of published systems
output only a binary fresh/spoiled label rather than a continuous
shelf-life estimate, which is the operationally useful quantity for
inventory management.

FoodSense addresses these gaps through four principal contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{On-edge drift compensation.} A closed-form temperature--humidity
        correction is computed directly on the ATmega328P microcontroller,
        eliminating post-hoc server-side recalibration.
  \item \textbf{Continuous shelf-life regression.} An attention-augmented LSTM
        replaces binary labels with a real-valued remaining shelf-life
        estimate in hours, enabling predictive inventory decisions.
  \item \textbf{Sub-\$5 virtual spectrophotometry.} A commodity RGB-LED/LDR
        assembly combined with an ML calibration MLP approximates a
        visible-range spectrophotometer, capturing pigment-level biomarkers
        without benchtop instrumentation.
  \item \textbf{Three-branch multimodal inference.} Gas, spectroscopic, and
        visual signals are processed by independent specialized models
        whose outputs are fused into a unified Freshness Score, improving
        robustness over any single modality.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Gas Sensor Drift and Compensation}
Metal-oxide gas sensors exhibit well-documented resistance drift
caused by temperature fluctuations, humidity absorption, and chemical
aging of the sensitive layer. Vergara \textit{et al.}~\cite{Vergara2012}
curated the widely used UCI Gas Sensor Array Drift Dataset to
benchmark adaptive correction algorithms under 36 months of
uncontrolled drift. Huerta \textit{et al.}~\cite{Huerta2016}
demonstrated that recurrent network adaptation can recover up to 84\%
of initial classification accuracy after one year of drift.
More recently, Yi \textit{et al.}~\cite{Yi2022} proposed a domain-
adaptation framework for cross-sensor drift compensation. FoodSense
departs from data-driven drift correction by deriving a closed-form
sensitivity correction that can be evaluated in $<$1~ms on a
8-bit AVR processor, enabling on-edge compensation without stored
reference datasets.

\subsection{Visible Spectroscopy for Food Quality}
Spectrophotometric analysis of food pigments---chlorophylls at 660~nm
and 430~nm, carotenoids near 540~nm---is an established laboratory
technique~\cite{Fodor2024}. Chang \textit{et al.}~\cite{Chang2023}
coupled an LED-based portable spectrometer with a CNN classifier to
grade citrus freshness, achieving 93\% accuracy. Guo \textit{et al.}~\cite{Guo2020}
demonstrated hyperspectral imaging for tomato bruise detection.
However, these systems rely on spectrometer hardware costing upwards
of USD 200. FoodSense replaces the spectrometer with a four-component
RGB-LED/LDR circuit whose signals are linearised by a calibration MLP
trained on KMnO$_4$ scanning data, reducing the spectroscopy subsystem
cost to under USD 5.

\subsection{Deep Learning for Visual Freshness Assessment}
Convolutional neural networks have been applied extensively to fruit
and vegetable freshness grading from RGB images. Cortes \textit{et al.}~\cite{Cortes2019}
achieved 91\% accuracy on a five-class tomato dataset using VGG-16.
Amin \textit{et al.}~\cite{Amin2023} applied transfer learning via
MobileNetV2 to a multi-produce freshness dataset, reporting 94\%
accuracy at 28~ms per inference on commodity hardware. Gao \textit{et al.}~\cite{Gao2024}
explored Vision Transformers for fine-grained produce defect
localisation, showing ViT-B/16 outperforms CNN baselines on texture-
rich degradation patterns. FoodSense employs both EfficientNet-B0 and
ViT-B/16 in a complementary dual-track visual branch.

\subsection{Temporal Modelling for Shelf-Life Forecasting}
Sequential modelling of gas sensor time-series has been investigated
for beverage fermentation monitoring~\cite{Ren2023} and meat spoilage
forecasting~\cite{Saha2023}. Nguyen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Nguyen2024}
demonstrated that LSTM networks with temporal attention outperform
vanilla RNNs for multi-step gas concentration forecasting. Wu \textit{et al.}~\cite{Wu2023}
coupled a spectrometer with an LSTM to predict remaining shelf life of
packaged salads with an RMSE of 3.8~hours. FoodSense extends this
approach by feeding compensated VOC, temperature, and humidity
readings into an attention-LSTM that is jointly conditioned on all
three environmental signals.

\subsection{Edge Hardware Node}

The hardware node (Fig.~\ref{fig:hw_pipeline}) comprises three
transducers interfaced to an Arduino Uno (ATmega328P, 16~MHz, 10-bit
ADC). The MQ135 metal-oxide sensor outputs an analog voltage
proportional to the sensor resistance $R_s$, which varies with the
concentration of the target VOC and is confounded by ambient
temperature $T$ and relative humidity $H$. The DHT11 digital
humidity-temperature sensor provides $T$ (accuracy $\pm$2$^{\circ}$C)
and $H$ (accuracy $\pm$5\% RH) at 1~Hz. The LDR is illuminated in
sequence by three primary-colour LEDs (red 620--750~nm, green
495--570~nm, blue 450--495~nm) driven by PWM-controlled GPIO pins;
transmitted intensity is digitised by the 10-bit ADC to form the
spectroscopic input vector.

\subsection{On-Edge Drift Compensation}

The MQ135 sensitivity is affected by ambient conditions according to
the manufacturer sensitivity correction curve. FoodSense implements a
closed-form linear approximation evaluated on the ATmega328P:

\begin{equation}
  \text{corr} = 1 + 0.02\,(T - 20) + 0.01\,(H - 65)
  \label{eq:corr}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  R_{s,\text{corr}} = \frac{R_s}{\text{corr}}
  \label{eq:rs_corr}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $T$ is temperature in $^{\circ}$C, $H$ is relative humidity in
\% RH, and the baseline conditions are 20$^{\circ}$C and 65\% RH.
The corrected resistance $R_{s,\text{corr}}$ is then converted to
VOC concentration via the power-law sensitivity model:

\begin{equation}
  C = A \left(\frac{R_{s,\text{corr}}}{R_0}\right)^{B}
  \label{eq:conc}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $R_0$ is the sensor resistance in clean air, and $A$, $B$ are
gas-specific calibration constants. This executes in under 1~ms on the
16~MHz AVR core, enabling a 1~Hz closed-loop sensing cycle.

\textbf{Gas Branch:} The MLP Gas Classifier and Attention-LSTM both

operating in parallel. The MLP provides a per-sample VOC-elevation flag;

\section{AI Models and Methodology}
\label{sec:models}

\subsection{MLP Gas Classifier}

\textbf{Dataset.} The UCI Gas Sensor Array Drift Dataset~\cite{Vergara2012}
contains 13,910 measurements collected over 36 months using a 16-MOX-
sensor array exposed to six pure gases. Each sample is described by
128 features: 8 statistical descriptors computed per sensor. For binary
food-spoilage detection, samples corresponding to ammonia, hydrogen
sulfide, and trimethylamine are labelled positive (VOC-elevated), and
the remaining gases serve as negative controls. The resulting class
distribution is approximately 7.5:1 (negative:positive), reflecting the
relative scarcity of spoilage-indicative gases in a balanced laboratory
dataset.

\textbf{Architecture.}
\begin{align*}
  &\text{Linear}(128,64) \to \text{BN} \to \text{ReLU}
   \to \text{Dropout}(0.3) \\
  &\to \text{Linear}(64,32) \to \text{BN} \to \text{ReLU}
   \to \text{Dropout}(0.2) \\
  &\to \text{Linear}(32,16) \to \text{ReLU}
   \to \text{Linear}(16,1)
\end{align*}
Loss: BCEWithLogitsLoss with \texttt{pos\_weight}~=~7.5 to counteract
class imbalance. Batch Normalisation (BN) is critical here: drift-affected
sensor data exhibit large inter-batch covariate shift, and BN standardises
internal activations to reduce sensitivity to the specific temporal
batch presented during training.

\textbf{Training.} Adam optimiser with $\text{lr}=10^{-3}$ and
weight decay $10^{-4}$, trained for 100 epochs on an 80/20 stratified
random split.

\subsection{Attention-Augmented LSTM for Shelf-Life Regression}

\textbf{Architecture.} A two-layer LSTM (hidden dim.~64, inter-layer
dropout~0.2) processes a rolling 30-reading window (1-hour context at
2-minute sampling) of $\mathbf{x}_t = [\text{VOC}_\text{ppm}, T, H, \Delta t]$.
The hidden states are passed through a Temporal Attention module:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{e} &= \mathbf{W}_a \mathbf{H} + \mathbf{b}_a \\
  \boldsymbol{\alpha} &= \text{softmax}(\mathbf{e}) \\
  \mathbf{c} &= \sum_{t=1}^{T} \alpha_t \mathbf{h}_t
\end{align}
The context $\mathbf{c}$ is decoded via
Linear$(64,32)$$\to$ReLU$\to$Linear$(32,1)$$\to\sigma \times 48$
to yield a shelf-life estimate in hours.

\textbf{Loss Function.} Huber loss ($\delta = 1.0$) is used instead of
MSE because spoilage curves exhibit plateaux punctuated by abrupt
transitions; MSE penalises large outlier errors quadratically,
destabilising gradient updates near transition points, whereas Huber
loss applies a linear penalty for errors exceeding $\delta$, improving
convergence on non-Gaussian residual distributions.

\textbf{Dataset and Training.} A synthetic 48-hour spoilage time-series
(1440 samples at 2-minute intervals, Arrhenius model, Gaussian noise
$\sigma{=}0.5$~h) is split chronologically 90/10. Adam with
ReduceLROnPlateau (patience~=~10, factor~=~0.5) and gradient clipping
(max\_norm~=~1.0); targets normalised to $[0, 1]$.

\subsection{Virtual Spectrometer Calibration MLP}

Visible absorbance follows Beer--Lambert law:
\begin{equation}
  A_\lambda = \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{I_0}{I}\right)
             = \varepsilon_\lambda \, c \, l
  \label{eq:beer}
\end{equation}
The Calibration MLP learns the inverse LDR-to-spectrum mapping:
\begin{align*}
  &\text{Lin}(4,64)\to\text{BN}\to\text{ReLU}\to\text{Drop}(0.2) \\
  &\to\text{Lin}(64,32)\to\text{BN}\to\text{ReLU}\to\text{Drop}(0.1) \\
  &\to\text{Lin}(32,2)
\end{align*}
Input: $(R_\text{raw}, G_\text{raw}, B_\text{raw}, \text{PWM})$;
output: $(\hat{\lambda}_\text{nm}, \hat{A})$. Multi-task loss:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \text{MSE}(\hat{\lambda}, \lambda)
                + 0.5\,\text{MSE}(\hat{A}, A)
  \label{eq:calib_loss}
\end{equation}
Training employs KMnO$_4$ scanning data, for which the extinction
spectrum is analytically known.

\subsection{Pigment Degradation Classifier}

Three wavelength-specific absorbance values are used, motivated by
established pigment photochemistry~\cite{Fodor2024}: $A_{430}$
(Chlorophyll~b Soret band), $A_{540}$ (carotenoid browning region),
and $A_{660}$ (Chlorophyll~a Q-band).
\begin{align*}
  &\text{Lin}(3,32)\to\text{BN}\to\text{ReLU}\to\text{Drop}(0.2) \\
  &\to\text{Lin}(32,16)\to\text{BN}\to\text{ReLU}\to\text{Lin}(16,3)
\end{align*}
CrossEntropyLoss over three classes: \textit{Fresh}
(chlorophyll-dominant), \textit{Ripe} (carotenoid-dominant), and
\textit{Spoiled} (browning-dominant).

\subsection{Visual Analysis Branch}

\textbf{EfficientNet-B0.} Pre-trained on ImageNet-21k; the final
classification head is replaced with a three-class linear layer. All
convolutional layers are fine-tuned with layer-wise learning rate
decay (classification head: $10^{-4}$; backbone: $10^{-5}$) to
preserve low-level texture features while adapting high-level
representations to produce freshness discrimination.

\textbf{Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16).} The ViT-B/16 model divides
each $224 \times 224$ image into $196$ non-overlapping $16{\times}16$
patches. The \texttt{[CLS]} token from the final transformer block feeds
a regression head for continuous quality scoring. Self-attention over
patch tokens captures global surface-texture patterns (mould spots,
discolouration gradients) that local convolution windows miss, making
ViT complementary to the CNN branch~\cite{Gao2024}.

The disparity between wavelength $R^2$ (0.9625) and absorbance $R^2$

this smooth mapping. Absorbance is computed from the LDR voltage ratio

across the visible spectrum, the signal-to-noise ratio for absorbance is
inherently heteroscedastic. A photodiode replacement is projected to
increase absorbance $R^2$ above 0.92 based on noise analysis.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{On-Edge Compensation}

Executing drift compensation on the ATmega328P rather than a remote
processing layer has two practical consequences. First, it eliminates
the need for timestamped temperature--humidity logging and retrospective
batch correction, enabling unattended sensor operation in cold-storage
environments with intermittent connectivity. Second, closed-form
evaluation (Eqs.~\ref{eq:corr}--\ref{eq:rs_corr}) produces a
deterministic corrected reading within the same 1-second sampling epoch,
suitable for real-time alarm triggering without server round-trips.

\subsection{Limitations}

Three principal limitations are acknowledged:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Synthetic LSTM training data.} The Arrhenius-derived
        time-series approximates bacterial respiration but does not capture
        species-specific variability or cold-chain interruptions. Real
        sensor logs with walk-forward cross-validation are required for
        deployment.
  \item \textbf{LDR noise.} Heteroscedastic Johnson--Nyquist noise limits
        absorbance $R^2$ to 0.7240. An APDS-9960 ambient-light sensor
        would reduce the noise floor by approximately 20~dB.
  \item \textbf{Single broadband gas sensor.} The MQ135 cannot discriminate
        between spoilage-specific VOCs and benign environmental gases
        (e.g.\ CO$_2$, alcohols) without cross-referencing other modalities.
        A 4--6 sensor MOX array would enable individual-gas deconvolution.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Work}

Planned extensions include: (i)~real spoilage time-series collection
across multiple produce categories (strawberry, spinach, chicken) under
ISO~4833-compliant microbiological validation; (ii)~a complementary gas
sensor array (MQ3 ethanol, MQ136 H$_2$S, MQ137 NH$_3$); (iii)~quantisation-
aware training for deployment on STM32 Cortex-M4 processors; and
(iv)~a multi-head attention fusion layer that learns adaptive weights
for the three inference branches as a function of environmental context.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented FoodSense, a multimodal IoT--AI framework for
early food spoilage detection integrating gas, spectroscopic, and
visual sensing into a unified sub-\$30 hardware node. Four core
technical contributions were demonstrated: closed-form on-edge drift
compensation for the MQ135 MOX sensor; an attention-augmented LSTM for
continuous shelf-life regression; a virtual RGB-LED/LDR spectrophotometer
calibrated via a multi-task MLP; and a three-branch deep inference
architecture combining MLP, LSTM, CNN, and Vision Transformer models.

Quantitative evaluation established: 96.84\% accuracy and 92.07\%
recall for VOC-elevation classification on the UCI Gas Sensor Drift
Dataset; $\pm$4.0~h MAE for shelf-life regression; 95.60\% accuracy
for pigment-based freshness grading; and a wavelength calibration $R^2$
of 0.9625. Relative to prior art, FoodSense uniquely combines gas,
spectroscopic, and visual modalities with continuous temporal forecasting
at low cost, addressing a capability gap identified across reviewed
studies.

Future work will address the synthetic data limitation through real
spoilage sensor collection, expand the gas-sensing array, and explore
model compression for full microcontroller deployment. FoodSense
provides a replicable foundation for affordable food quality monitoring
in supply chains where laboratory instrumentation is unavailable.
